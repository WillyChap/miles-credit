# Lessons Learned: Hard conservation correctors placed inside the training loss create optimizer-exploitable feedback in AI weather and climate emulators

## Abstract

Data-driven emulators of the atmosphere increasingly enforce physical conservation laws through correction layers that adjust the network output so that a global budget closes. We report a failure mode that arises when such a corrector is applied during training and the supervised loss is computed on the corrected output. In a global climate emulator, a water-budget corrector that rescales precipitation to close the global moisture balance produced a positive feedback loop: the optimizer learned to under-predict raw precipitation and let the corrector inflate it, driving the global water-budget drift from near zero to roughly 23 percent over twelve epochs. The corrected output still closed the budget, so a diagnostic computed on the corrected field reported success while the underlying prediction degraded. We identify the cause as gradient flow through the corrector and the absence of any loss term rewarding genuine closure. The remedy is a hybrid constraint. A hard correction continues to guarantee exact closure of the delivered field, while the same budget imbalance enters the training loss as a soft penalty evaluated on the uncorrected prediction, so gradients never pass through the corrector. With this decoupling the drift returned to near zero within one epoch and held there for sixty-four epochs. The lesson generalizes to any AI weather or climate emulator that combines a hard physical-constraint correction with end-to-end training: hard correctors and soft penalties enforce the same constraint in different places, and only the soft penalty belongs inside the gradient path to the supervised target.

## 1. Introduction

Neural network emulators of weather and climate now match or exceed traditional numerical models on several deterministic forecast metrics, yet they do not inherit the conservation laws that physical models satisfy by construction. A trained network can produce fields that violate global mass, water, or energy budgets even when its pointwise error is small (Beucler et al. 2021). Two broad strategies address this. The first enforces constraints as soft penalties: a term proportional to the constraint violation is added to the loss, and the network is rewarded for reducing it (Beucler et al. 2021, their loss-constrained networks). The second enforces constraints as hard corrections: a deterministic operator projects or rescales the network output so the constraint holds exactly, either inside the architecture or as a post-processing layer (Beucler et al. 2021, their architecture-constrained networks; Sha et al. 2025). The two are not exclusive. A hard correction can guarantee exact closure of the delivered field while a soft penalty shapes what the network learns, a hybrid we return to in Section 4.

Hard correctors are attractive because they guarantee closure to machine precision and require no weight tuning. In the CREDIT modeling framework (Schreck et al. 2025), global mass, water, and energy budgets are closed by post-block correctors that adjust surface pressure, precipitation, and atmospheric energy after the network forward pass (Sha et al. 2025). These correctors were designed for inference, where the network weights are fixed and the correction simply cleans up the final field.

We report what happens when one such hard corrector is left active during training and the supervised loss sees the corrected output. The result is a degenerate solution that the optimizer actively seeks. We describe the mechanism, quantify the drift it produces, present two fixes, and state the conditions under which the failure should be expected. The finding applies to the class of AI weather and climate emulators that pair a hard physical-constraint correction with gradient-based end-to-end training.

## 2. Setup

The emulator is CAMulator, a global atmospheric model trained to reproduce 6-hourly states of the Community Atmosphere Model (CAM6) on a 1.0 degree grid with 32 hybrid sigma levels. Prognostic fields include winds, temperature, total specific humidity, surface pressure, and surface precipitation. Training minimizes a latitude- and variable-weighted error between the predicted and target next state.

The water-budget corrector, the GlobalWaterFixer, enforces global moisture conservation. At each step it computes three globally integrated, area-weighted quantities: the tendency of total column water content, the global evaporation source, and the global precipitation sink. Conservation requires that the storage tendency equal the net of source and sink. The corrector forms the residual of this balance and rescales the predicted precipitation field by the multiplicative factor that would close it. We denote the closure factor r, so that a value of r equal to 1 indicates a budget that already closes and the corrector does nothing. The fractional drift we report is r minus 1, expressed as a percentage; it equals the budget residual divided by the global precipitation sink.

The reference for a closed budget comes from the training data itself. Integrating the same three quantities with the same area weights and the same physics over one year of CAM6 output (1459 6-hourly steps, calendar year 1980) gives a mean drift of 0.024 percent with a standard deviation of 2.22 percent, and a global precipitation sink that balances the global evaporation source at 1.72 times 10 to the 10 kg per second with near-zero net storage. The data close the global water budget to within a few hundredths of a percent in the mean. Any persistent model drift of order 10 percent is therefore a property of the model, not of the target.

## 3. The failure: a feedback loop the optimizer exploits

The corrector was enabled during a fine-tuning phase, applied to the predicted state before the loss was computed. In code terms, the corrector rescaled precipitation in place and the supervised error was then evaluated on the rescaled field. Over twelve epochs the global water-budget drift rose monotonically from about 2 percent to a plateau near 23 percent (Fig. 1, red). The target value is 0.024 percent. A second, independent training run in the same configuration reproduced the failure and drifted further, to roughly 45 percent, which shows the behavior is a property of the configuration rather than of a single run.

The mechanism is a closed feedback loop between the corrector and the optimizer. The corrector multiplies predicted precipitation by the closure factor r. When raw precipitation is too low to close the budget, r exceeds 1 and the corrector scales precipitation up. The loss, computed on the corrected field, then sees precipitation that slightly overshoots the target and produces a gradient that lowers raw precipitation further. Lower raw precipitation enlarges the residual, which raises r, which scales the next prediction up more. The system converges to a degenerate equilibrium in which raw precipitation is far too low, the closure factor is large, and the corrected precipitation sits near the target. At the 23 percent plateau the corrector was inflating precipitation by roughly a quarter, implying a raw precipitation sink about 18 percent below the reference.

Two design choices made this possible. First, gradients flowed through the corrector: because the loss was a function of the corrected output, and the correction depended on the network output through r, the optimizer could reduce the loss by manipulating the input to the corrector rather than by improving the physics. Second, nothing in the loss rewarded genuine closure. The corrector computed the budget imbalance but the training objective never included it, so a large closure factor carried no penalty.

This failure is dangerous because it hides. A diagnostic computed on the corrected field, the quantity a user would naturally monitor, reported a closed budget throughout, since closure is what the corrector guarantees by construction. The degradation was visible only in the closure factor itself and in the raw, pre-correction precipitation. Evaluating the corrected output confirms only that the corrector ran.

## 4. The fix: keep the corrector out of the gradient path, and penalize the imbalance

Three responses are available, and they are not equivalent.

The first is to disable the corrector during training and apply it only at inference. This removes the feedback loop and is the least invasive option. It forgoes any benefit of training-time conservation, and prior work on physics-constrained emulators reports that conservation learned during training can improve fields beyond what an inference-only correction achieves [CITE: FuXi physics-constraint ablation]. We did not measure that trade-off here.

The second response keeps the corrector in place but stops gradients from flowing through it. The supervised loss is computed on the pre-correction prediction, so the optimizer drives raw precipitation toward the target directly and never optimizes against the corrector. The budget imbalance is then added as a separate penalty, proportional to the squared closure deviation. Because the closure factor minus one equals the budget residual divided by the global precipitation sink, this penalty is the normalized global imbalance of the network's uncorrected output. The soft penalty and the hard corrector then enforce the same constraint on the same field, differing only in that the penalty rewards closure while the corrector imposes it. This is a hybrid of the loss-constrained and architecture-constrained formulations of Beucler et al. (2021): the soft penalty shapes the learned solution during training, and the hard corrector is retained to guarantee exact closure of the delivered field at inference. Structurally it cannot juice the residual, because no path exists from the closure factor to the supervised gradient. In a continuation run from the degenerate checkpoint, at a penalty weight of 0.1, the decoupling returned the drift from 23 percent to below 1 percent within a single epoch and held it near zero for the following sixty-four epochs, with a mean of 0.11 percent and a standard deviation of 0.83 percent over the final five epochs.

The third response computes the supervised loss on the corrected output, as in the failing configuration, but adds the budget imbalance as an explicit penalty and, on multi-step rollout, penalizes the imbalance at intermediate steps as well. The intent is that the penalty outweighs the feedback rather than removing it. We have one training run in this configuration, and it was unstable: the drift oscillated between roughly minus 30 and plus 15 percent for about twenty epochs, one branch diverged to several hundred percent with non-finite losses, and the run recovered only after a restart, settling near 1 percent with a wider spread than the decoupled run. That run also trained from a random initialization and logged unrelated non-finite-loss events, and its penalty weight was not recorded, so the instability cannot be attributed to the post-correction coupling alone. We therefore report this configuration as untested rather than as a working alternative; a controlled comparison against the second response remains to be run.

We recommend the second response as the principled solution. It is the only configuration we have shown to be stable, and by construction it removes the feedback path rather than outweighing it. What separates it from the failing configuration is two changes: the budget imbalance enters the loss as a penalty rather than being computed and discarded, and the supervised gradient is kept off the corrected field. The penalty is what converts the corrector from a quantity the optimizer games into a constraint the optimizer respects.

Two implementation details proved necessary for the diagnosis rather than the fix. Global budget integrals must be accumulated in double precision; summing roughly 55,000 single-precision grid-cell contributions introduced a rounding error of about 0.6 percent, comparable to the natural per-step spread of the target and large enough to obscure small genuine drifts. And the corrector must run as an explicit step whose imbalance is returned to the training loop, rather than as an opaque in-line operation, so that the penalty term can be formed at all.

## 5. The generalizable lesson

Hard correctors and soft penalties enforce the same constraint in different places, and the placement determines whether end-to-end training is stable. A soft penalty acts on the loss surface: it adds a gradient that points toward the constraint, and the network moves with it. A hard corrector acts on the output: it removes the constraint violation after the fact, and if that corrected output then feeds the loss, the corrector becomes part of the computational graph the optimizer searches. The optimizer will exploit any degree of freedom the corrector exposes, including the freedom to offload error onto the correction.

The lesson for AI weather and climate emulators is therefore specific and actionable. A hard physical-constraint correction may be used freely at inference, but during training it must be kept out of the gradient path between the network and the supervised target. If a constraint is to influence training, it should enter as a penalty on the constraint violation, which is the soft formulation, not as a correction of the field that the supervised loss evaluates. The two strategies can be combined, with the corrector guaranteeing exact closure of the delivered field and the penalty shaping the learned solution, provided the supervised loss is taken on the uncorrected prediction. This hybrid is, in short, a hard constraint that is trained softly. The two parts do not act in the same place at the same time. Under the decoupling, the hard correction contributes nothing to the gradient during training and matters only for the delivered field and at inference, while the soft penalty carries all of the constraint's influence on learning.

A corollary concerns evaluation. A conservation diagnostic computed on a hard-corrected field is uninformative about the model, because closure is imposed by construction. The honest diagnostics are the magnitude of the correction the model requires and the physical fidelity of the pre-correction field. Reporting only the corrected budget can present a degrading model as a conserving one.

These points apply wherever a deterministic projection or rescaling enforces a global invariant on a trained emulator: mass and surface-pressure fixers, energy-balance fixers, moisture fixers, and tracer non-negativity clamps all expose a correction factor that an end-to-end optimizer can manipulate if the loss sees the corrected output.

## 6. Limitations

The evidence comes from a single emulator, a single physical constraint, and a small number of training configurations rather than a controlled comparison across models and constraints. The drift trajectory and the recovery are drawn from training-log diagnostics of production runs, not from a designed experiment with held-out evaluation. The decoupled penalty formulation was stable over sixty-four training epochs, but this is drift measured per training step on teacher-forced data; we have not verified that the budget remains closed over a long free-running climate integration, where slow drift would matter most. The post-correction variant rests on a single unstable run and remains untested. We report the behavior of the global water budget and its closure, and deliberately do not claim an improvement in forecast skill or climate fidelity, which require separate evaluation. The reference budget is one calendar year; whether its few-percent per-step spread is stationary across years is unverified. Finally, we did not measure the inference-only baseline against the training-time penalty, so the claim that training-time conservation helps rests on prior work rather than on results presented here.
