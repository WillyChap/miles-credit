\title{Hard conservation correctors can hide scale degeneracy during AI weather and climate model training}

\authors{William E. Chapman\aff{a}\correspondingauthor{William E. Chapman, wich6609@colorado.edu}

\affiliation{\aff{a}{Department of Atmospheric and Oceanic Sciences, University of Colorado Boulder, Boulder, Colorado}}

\abstract{We enabled a water-budget corrector during the training of a global climate emulator, expecting a more physically faithful model. Every delivered field closed the moisture budget to machine precision; yet over 12 epochs the raw precipitation developed a growing global low bias, and the correction needed to hide it grew from about 2\% to roughly 24\%. A diagnostic evaluated after correction therefore reported success while the raw prediction deteriorated. The cause is a scale degeneracy. The corrector rescales precipitation to whatever total closes the budget, so uniformly scaling the raw field is undone exactly by the inverse change in the correction factor and is invisible to a loss evaluated on the corrected output. This invariance does not by itself drive the drift; it removes the restoring force that would otherwise oppose it, leaving raw amplitude free to follow other optimization pressures. A combined remedy supervises the uncorrected prediction, adds the raw budget imbalance as a soft penalty, and retains the hard correction only for the delivered field; the required correction returned to within one percent in a single epoch and stayed there for 64 epochs, although an ablation isolating the two changes is still underway. The broader lesson: a budget that closes perfectly after correction confirms only that the corrector ran, not that the model beneath it is sound.}

\statement
AI weather and climate models increasingly use deterministic correctors to close global budgets exactly. We show that such a corrector can quietly remove a real degree of freedom, global precipitation amplitude, from the training objective: the raw model can then drift while the delivered field stays exactly conservative, because the corrector closes the model's own predicted moisture budget regardless of that amplitude. A remedy that restored stability is to supervise the uncorrected prediction, penalize its budget imbalance, and keep the hard correction only for the field users receive. Conservation should also be evaluated before correction, because a budget checked after a hard correction confirms only that the corrector ran.

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Neural network emulators of weather and climate can match traditional numerical models on several deterministic forecast metrics \citep{bi2023,lam2023,kochkov2024}, yet they do not inherit physical conservation laws by construction. A network can violate global mass, water, or energy budgets even when its pointwise error is small \citep{beucler2021,sha2025}. Soft constraints address this by adding the violation to the loss, whereas hard constraints project or rescale the output so that the constraint holds exactly \citep{beucler2021,zanetta2023,valente2025,wattmeyer2025,sha2025}. The strategies can be combined: a soft penalty can shape the learned prediction while a hard correction guarantees closure of the delivered field \citep{beucler2021,sha2025}.

Hard correctors are attractive because they guarantee closure to machine precision without tuning a penalty weight. In the CREDIT framework, global mass, water, and energy budgets are closed by correctors that adjust surface pressure, precipitation, and atmospheric energy after the network forward pass \citep{schreck2025,sha2025}. Allowing the supervised loss to see the corrected output also appears reasonable: the network is optimized against the field that users ultimately receive, every training sample satisfies the intended budget, and prior studies show that conservation imposed during training can improve stability and precipitation behavior \citep{wattmeyer2025,sha2025}. We therefore expected that leaving the water corrector active during fine-tuning would make the learned model more physically faithful. Instead, the corrected budget appeared perfect while the raw precipitation prediction developed a growing low bias. The resulting investigation revealed a scale degeneracy hidden by the correction. We describe how the failure presented itself, why the usual conservation diagnostic missed it, and how a revised objective restored stable behavior.

\section{Setup}\label{sec:setup}

CAMulator is a global atmospheric emulator trained to reproduce 6-hourly Community Atmosphere Model (CAM6) states on a $1.0^\circ$ grid with 32 hybrid sigma levels. Prognostic fields include winds, temperature, total specific humidity, surface pressure, and surface precipitation. Training minimizes a latitude- and variable-weighted error between the predicted and target next state. The experiments below come from a fine-tuning phase in which the decoder blocks that emit every prognostic and diagnostic field, precipitation included, were trainable while the backbone was frozen; optimization used Adam at a fixed learning rate of $2.5\times10^{-5}$ with no weight decay.

The water corrector computes the globally integrated tendency of total column water, evaporation source, and precipitation sink. It then rescales predicted precipitation by the factor required to close the budget. We denote this factor $r$: $r=1$ means the raw prediction already closes, and the fractional correction is $r-1$. With evaporation and precipitation defined as positive source and sink magnitudes, respectively, the residual and sign convention are given in the appendix.

Applying the same calculation to one year of CAM6 output (1459 6-hourly steps in 1980) gives a mean correction of 0.024\% with a standard deviation of 2.22\%. The global precipitation sink balances evaporation at $1.72\times10^{10}$~kg~s$^{-1}$ with near-zero mean storage. Persistent model corrections of order 10\% therefore do not originate in the target data.

\section{A perfectly conserved output hides a growing raw-precipitation bias}\label{sec:failure}

During fine-tuning, the corrector rescaled predicted precipitation in place before the supervised error was evaluated. By the intended physical diagnostic, the change initially looked successful: the delivered field closed the global water budget to machine precision at every step. The warning appeared in a quantity that was not part of that pass--fail test. Over 12 epochs, the correction required to obtain closure trended upward from about 2\% to roughly 24\% (Fig.~\ref{fig:drift}), while the raw global precipitation sink fell to about 18\% below the CAM6 reference (Fig.~\ref{fig:drift}c). The model was still delivering an exactly conservative field, but only because the corrector was compensating for an increasingly biased raw prediction.

Several checks narrowed the cause. The same budget calculation applied to CAM6 showed near-zero mean imbalance, so the trajectory did not originate in the target data. A second training run in the same configuration reproduced the behavior and drifted further, with per-step corrections exceeding 60\%, ruling out a single anomalous optimization path. The mass and energy correctors remained near 0.01\% while the water correction grew (Fig.~\ref{fig:fixers}), indicating that the model had not simply become unstable in every conserved quantity. Finally, recomputing the global reductions at higher precision changed the correction by only tenths of a percent, far too little to explain a 24\% signal. Together, these checks pointed to the coupling between the precipitation corrector and the supervised loss.

The failure was therefore both physical and diagnostic. The raw precipitation amplitude developed a low bias, but the post-correction conservation metric could not reveal it because exact closure was guaranteed by construction; evaluating the corrected budget confirmed only that the corrector ran. The combination of a growing correction, a nearly unchanged delivered field, and stable mass and energy corrections suggested that the water corrector had not merely repaired an error; it had removed information that the supervised objective needed to identify.

\section{Why the supervised loss could not see the degradation}\label{sec:mechanism}

The corrector rescales raw precipitation to whatever global total the budget demands. If the network multiplied its entire precipitation field by two, the corrector would simply halve its correction factor and deliver the identical output: the two changes cancel exactly, and a loss that sees only the corrected field cannot tell whether the raw amplitude was right, doubled, or halved. Formally, let $P$ be the raw precipitation field and $Q$ the global precipitation total required by the predicted storage tendency and evaporation. The corrected field is
\begin{equation}
C(P)=Q\frac{P}{\mathbf{1}^{T}P},\qquad C(cP)=C(P),\qquad J_C(P)P=0.
\label{eq:null}
\end{equation}
The raw-amplitude direction therefore lies in the null space of the correction Jacobian, and a supervised loss evaluated only on $C(P)$ supplies no gradient that identifies the amplitude of $P$.

We confirmed the invariance numerically on real CAM6 precipitation fields rather than relying on the algebra alone. Applying uniform scale factors $c\in\{0.5,0.75,1.0,1.25,1.5\}$ to the raw field, the corrected output $C(cP)$ was unchanged to machine precision (the maximum relative difference was $2\times10^{-16}$ in double precision and $10^{-7}$ in single precision), while the correction factor scaled as $r\propto 1/c$ and the raw-output precipitation loss varied quadratically about its minimum at $c=1$. The corrected-output loss was flat across the range, and only the imbalance penalty $(r-1)^2$ responded to the scaling, changing by roughly three orders of magnitude. A finite-difference probe along the raw-amplitude direction, $[C(P+\epsilon P)-C(P)]/\epsilon$, did not settle to a fixed value as $\epsilon\to0$ but grew as $1/\epsilon$, the fingerprint of a derivative that is exactly zero (only rounding survives in the numerator), not merely small. The test isolates the analytic invariance for precipitation alone; in training the network changes precipitation, evaporation, and storage together, so the optimizer's path is not confined to this exact direction. Still, the corrected-output loss carries no information about raw precipitation amplitude, leaving the imbalance penalty as the only term that can constrain it.

The invariance removes the restoring force on raw amplitude; it does not supply one. A flat direction permits motion but cannot by itself produce the sign-consistent ramp we observed, so some pressure outside the corrected-output loss must drive it. We did not isolate that pressure, but two candidates are consistent with a downward push under a frozen backbone. Precipitation is emitted by the same decoder blocks that emit the strongly supervised prognostic fields, so gradients that improve those fields perturb shared weights and can move precipitation amplitude with nothing to oppose it. Mean-squared-error training on a non-negative, heavy-tailed precipitation field can also favor under-prediction, because displaced precipitation is penalized at both the predicted and the observed location \citep{gilleland2009,subich2025}. Weight decay, the most obvious sign-consistent shrinking force, was disabled in these runs and can be excluded. The drift was not self-limiting at a fixed value (a second run drifted past 60\%), so we do not read the apparent plateau as a mechanism-determined magnitude. It more likely marks the point where continued change ceases to be a uniform rescaling and begins to alter the normalized spatial pattern or other jointly predicted fields, restoring opposing gradients.

The contrast among correctors helps explain why the mathematical vulnerability became visible only for water. Mass and energy adjustments act on temperature, humidity, and surface pressure, fields supervised across many levels or through other coupled terms. The water adjustment acts on precipitation, whose global amplitude became weakly identified after correction. A null direction is therefore not sufficient to produce runaway behavior; it must also overlap a degree of freedom that the remaining objective does not strongly restore.

\section{The fix: supervise the raw field and penalize its imbalance}\label{sec:fix}

One response is to disable the corrector during training and apply it only at inference. This removes the degeneracy from the training graph but gives up any benefit of teaching the network to conserve \citep{beucler2021,sha2025}. We did not evaluate that trade-off.

Our stable configuration follows directly from the diagnosis and gives each component one job. We supervise the raw, pre-correction prediction, so the loss again feels precipitation amplitude directly. We add the squared raw closure deviation as a soft penalty, teaching the network to close its own water budget. We keep the hard corrector for a single purpose: guaranteeing exact closure of the delivered field (Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic}). No supervised gradient passes through corrected precipitation, so the corrector can no longer conceal raw amplitude error from the target loss; constraint information now reaches the network only through the explicit penalty on the uncorrected prediction.

Starting from the degenerate checkpoint, this combined formulation, with penalty weight 0.1, reduced the required correction from 24\% to below 1\% within one epoch and held it near zero for the next 64 epochs; over the final five epochs the mean correction was 0.4\% with a standard deviation of 0.9\%. Because the soft penalty targets $(r-1)^2$ directly, the collapse of the required correction is a necessary but not sufficient sign of a healthier model; the more independent signal is the recovery of the raw precipitation sink toward the CAM6 reference (Fig.~\ref{fig:drift}c). We did not measure raw-field forecast skill. Because supervised-loss placement and the penalty were changed together, these runs establish stability but do not isolate the contribution of each change; that requires the $2\times2$ ablation identified in Section~\ref{sec:limits}, which is in progress. % TODO(2x2): cells B (corrected loss + penalty) and C (raw loss, no penalty) are running; once done, report which intervention is sufficient and convert this caveat + Fig 1b into a 4-cell result.

Two implementation details aided diagnosis. Global reductions should not be accumulated at bfloat16 precision: across 30 sampled steps, bfloat16 introduced correction errors averaging 0.22\% and reaching 0.52\%. This is a monitoring hazard rather than a cause of the failure: the errors are some fifty times smaller than the documented drift (Section~\ref{sec:failure}). It can still corrupt the diagnostic used to detect drift. Float32 was adequate in our tests, and we used float64 to make numerical error negligible. The corrector must also return the raw imbalance explicitly so that it can be monitored and included in the loss rather than hidden inside an in-place operation.

\section{The generalizable lesson}\label{sec:lesson}

A hard corrector need not be excluded from every differentiable architecture \citep{beucler2021,zanetta2023,valente2025}. The specific danger occurs when the corrected field is the sole supervised target and the correction operator has a nontrivial null direction corresponding to a physically meaningful property of the raw prediction. The loss then cannot identify that property unless another term constrains it.

This yields a general diagnostic beyond multiplicative precipitation correction. Let $C(\hat y)$ be a differentiable correction and let the supervised objective be $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sup}}(y,C(\hat y))$. Any perturbation $v$ satisfying $J_C(\hat y)v=0$ is locally invisible to that objective because
\begin{equation}
\left.\frac{d}{d\epsilon}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sup}}\!\left(y,C(\hat y+\epsilon v)\right)\right|_{\epsilon=0}
=\nabla_C\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sup}}^{T}J_C(\hat y)v=0.
\label{eq:generalnull}
\end{equation}
If $v$ represents a physically meaningful property and no other term identifies it, the raw model is nonidentifiable in that direction. The pretraining question is therefore not only whether the corrected field satisfies the constraint, but which information the correction removes from the learning problem.

The practical test is to examine what information the correction removes. For a multiplicative global rescaling, Eq.~\eqref{eq:null} shows that raw amplitude is invisible after correction. During training, that amplitude must be constrained independently, for example by supervising the raw field or by adding an appropriate penalty. In our recommended hybrid, the raw supervised loss identifies precipitation amplitude, the soft penalty pushes the raw prediction toward conservation, and the hard corrector guarantees exact closure of the delivered field. Corrected-output supervision combined with a sufficiently strong penalty might also control the degeneracy, but it would be tuning dependent and was not established here.

The result applies most directly to deterministic rescalings and projections with null spaces that overlap weakly supervised model degrees of freedom, including global moisture, energy, mass, and surface-pressure fixers. It should not be generalized automatically to every constraint operator; the relevant question is whether the operator removes information that the rest of the objective fails to restore.

Evaluation must follow the same logic. A conservation diagnostic computed after a hard correction is fixed by construction and says nothing about the learned model, so training and evaluation should separately track the pre-correction residual, the magnitude of the required correction, raw-field skill, and corrected-field skill. The correction factor is itself a model diagnostic: a growing correction can reveal deterioration even while the delivered field remains exactly conservative. A budget that closes perfectly after correction confirms only that the corrector ran, not that the model beneath it is sound.

\section{Relation to prior work}\label{sec:prior}

Our result complements \citet{sha2025}, who report benefits from global conservation during training and inference, including reduced precipitation bias and improved long-range stability. Their work establishes that training-time conservation can help; ours identifies a coupling that can make it fail when the corrected output is the only supervised target.

ACE2 provides a close and instructive comparison \citep{wattmeyer2025}. Its corrector multiplies precipitation by a globally uniform factor to close the moisture budget (the same operator we analyze) and is applied before a mean-squared-error loss with no conservation penalty. ACE2 reports stable long integrations, with no drift in total atmospheric moisture and a realistic precipitation climatology. These diagnostics are computed after correction, however, and do not include the correction factor or the raw precipitation field; by the mechanism described here, they cannot by themselves establish whether raw precipitation amplitude is stable. We do not claim that ACE2 either suffers from or avoids this degeneracy, only that its published diagnostics cannot settle the question, which is exactly the post-correction blind spot we document. Reporting the correction factor would resolve it, and is the same practice we recommend for training and evaluation.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}

The evidence comes from one emulator, one physical constraint, and production training logs rather than a designed cross-model experiment. The stable run changed both supervised-loss placement and penalty weight, so a controlled $2\times2$ comparison, now in progress, is required: corrected versus raw supervision, each with and without the imbalance penalty. Such an experiment would determine whether raw supervision alone is sufficient and whether a penalty can stabilize corrected-output supervision.

The reported correction is measured per teacher-forced training step. We have not tested long free-running climate integrations, nor do we claim improved forecast skill or climate fidelity. The stable configuration remained near zero for 64 epochs, but longer integrations are required to assess accumulated drift. The CAM6 reference is stationary across 1980--84, with annual mean corrections from 0.022\% to 0.029\% and standard deviations from 2.04\% to 2.29\%. Finally, we did not compare inference-only correction against training-time penalization, so any broader skill benefit rests on prior work rather than the experiments reported here.

\appendixtitle{The conservation-coupled training loss}

Let $\hat y$ be the network prediction and $y$ the target next state. The supervised term is a latitude- and variable-weighted error,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sup}}(y,\hat y)=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{v=1}^{V}\frac{1}{BHW}\sum_{b,i,j}w_vL_i^{\mathrm{lat}}\ell\!\left(y_{b,v,ij},\hat y_{b,v,ij}\right),
\end{equation}
where $L_i^{\mathrm{lat}}=\cos(\varphi_i)^p/\overline{\cos(\varphi)^p}$ and $\ell$ is mean squared error.

For each sample $b$, the water corrector forms globally integrated total-column-water tendency, positive evaporation source, and positive precipitation sink,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{T}_b=\!\sum_{ij}a_{ij}\frac{\mathrm{TWC}^{\mathrm{pred}}_{b,ij}-\mathrm{TWC}^{\mathrm{in}}_{b,ij}}{\Delta t},\quad
\mathcal{E}_b=\!\sum_{ij}a_{ij}\frac{E_{b,ij}\rho_w}{\Delta t},\quad
\mathcal{P}_b=\!\sum_{ij}a_{ij}\frac{P_{b,ij}\rho_w}{\Delta t}.
\end{equation}
Closure requires $\mathcal{T}_b=\mathcal{E}_b-\mathcal{P}_b$. The residual and precipitation correction factor are therefore
\begin{equation}
R_b=\mathcal{E}_b-\mathcal{T}_b-\mathcal{P}_b,\qquad
r_b=\frac{\mathcal{P}_b+R_b}{\mathcal{P}_b}=\frac{\mathcal{E}_b-\mathcal{T}_b}{\mathcal{P}_b},\qquad
r_b-1=\frac{R_b}{\mathcal{P}_b}.
\end{equation}
The corrector applies $P\!\to\!r_bP$. The soft penalty is the normalized raw imbalance,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{water}}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}(r_b-1)^2=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\left(\frac{R_b}{\mathcal{P}_b}\right)^2.
\end{equation}
The stable objective is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sup}}\!\left(y,\hat y_{\mathrm{pre}}\right)+\lambda_w\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{water}},
\label{eq:total}
\end{equation}
where $\hat y_{\mathrm{pre}}$ is the pre-correction prediction. Evaluating the supervised term only on corrected precipitation removes the uniform-amplitude direction from that term, as shown by Eq.~\eqref{eq:null}; it does not by itself choose the direction of drift. Equation~\eqref{eq:total} restores direct supervision of raw amplitude and adds a gradient toward raw budget closure. Mass and energy correctors compute analogous factors but contribute no penalty here.
