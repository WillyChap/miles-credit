\title{Lessons Learned: Hard conservation correctors placed inside the training
loss create optimizer-exploitable feedback in AI weather and climate emulators}

\authors{William E. Chapman\aff{a}\correspondingauthor{William E. Chapman, wich6609@colorado.edu}
% and Coauthors\aff{a}     % TODO: add coauthors
}

\affiliation{\aff{a}{National Center for Atmospheric Research, Boulder, Colorado}}

\abstract{Data-driven emulators of the atmosphere increasingly enforce physical
conservation laws through correction layers that adjust the network output so that a
global budget closes. We report a failure mode that arises when such a corrector is
applied during training and the supervised loss is computed on the corrected output.
In a global climate emulator, a water-budget corrector that rescales precipitation to
close the global moisture balance produced a positive feedback loop: the optimizer
learned to under-predict raw precipitation and let the corrector inflate it, driving
the global water-budget drift from near zero to roughly 23\% over 12 epochs. The
corrected output still closed the budget, so a diagnostic computed on the corrected
field reported success while the underlying prediction degraded. We identify the cause
as gradient flow through the corrector and the absence of any loss term rewarding
genuine closure. The remedy is a hybrid constraint: a hard correction continues to
guarantee exact closure of the delivered field, while the same budget imbalance enters
the training loss as a soft penalty evaluated on the uncorrected prediction, so
gradients never pass through the corrector. With this decoupling the drift returned to
near zero within one epoch and held there for 64 epochs. The lesson generalizes to any
AI weather or climate emulator that combines a hard physical-constraint correction with
end-to-end training: hard correctors and soft penalties enforce the same constraint in
different places, and only the soft penalty belongs inside the gradient path to the
supervised target.}

\statement
A growing number of AI weather and climate models bolt a physical correction onto the
network so that conserved quantities, such as global water or energy, balance exactly.
We show that leaving such a correction inside the training loop, where the loss is
measured on the corrected output, lets the optimizer cheat: it learns a physically wrong
prediction and relies on the correction to hide it, while standard conservation
diagnostics still look perfect. The fix is to keep the hard correction out of the
gradient path and instead add the imbalance as a soft penalty on the uncorrected
prediction. The practical lesson is simple and transferable: enforce constraints hard on
the delivered field but soft in the loss.

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Neural network emulators of weather and climate now match or exceed traditional
numerical models on several deterministic forecast metrics, yet they do not inherit the
conservation laws that physical models satisfy by construction. A trained network can
produce fields that violate global mass, water, or energy budgets even when its
pointwise error is small \citep{beucler2021}. Two broad strategies address this. The
first enforces constraints as soft penalties: a term proportional to the constraint
violation is added to the loss, and the network is rewarded for reducing it
\citep[their loss-constrained networks]{beucler2021}. The second enforces constraints as
hard corrections: a deterministic operator projects or rescales the network output so
the constraint holds exactly, either inside the architecture or as a post-processing
layer \citep[their architecture-constrained networks]{beucler2021}; \citep{sha2025}. The
two are not exclusive. A hard correction can guarantee exact closure of the delivered
field while a soft penalty shapes what the network learns, a hybrid we return to in
section~\ref{sec:fix}.

Hard correctors are attractive because they guarantee closure to machine precision and
require no weight tuning. In the CREDIT modeling framework \citep{schreck2025}, global
mass, water, and energy budgets are closed by post-block correctors that adjust surface
pressure, precipitation, and atmospheric energy after the network forward pass
\citep{sha2025}. These correctors were designed for inference, where the network weights
are fixed and the correction simply cleans up the final field.

We report what happens when one such hard corrector is left active during training and
the supervised loss sees the corrected output. The result is a degenerate solution that
the optimizer actively seeks. We describe the mechanism, quantify the drift it produces,
present the fixes, and state the conditions under which the failure should be expected.
The finding applies to the class of AI weather and climate emulators that pair a hard
physical-constraint correction with gradient-based end-to-end training.

\section{Setup}\label{sec:setup}

The emulator is CAMulator, a global atmospheric model trained to reproduce 6-hourly
states of the Community Atmosphere Model (CAM6) on a $1.0^\circ$ grid with 32 hybrid
sigma levels. Prognostic fields include winds, temperature, total specific humidity,
surface pressure, and surface precipitation. Training minimizes a latitude- and
variable-weighted error between the predicted and target next state.

The water-budget corrector enforces global moisture conservation. At each step it
computes three globally integrated, area-weighted quantities: the tendency of total
column water content, the global evaporation source, and the global precipitation sink.
Conservation requires that the storage tendency equal the net of source and sink. The
corrector forms the residual of this balance and rescales the predicted precipitation
field by the multiplicative factor that would close it. We denote the closure factor
$r$, so that $r=1$ indicates a budget that already closes and the corrector does
nothing. The fractional drift we report is $r-1$, expressed as a percentage; it equals
the budget residual divided by the global precipitation sink.

The reference for a closed budget comes from the training data itself. Integrating the
same three quantities with the same area weights and the same physics over one year of
CAM6 output (1459 6-hourly steps, calendar year 1980) gives a mean drift of 0.024\% with
a standard deviation of 2.22\%, and a global precipitation sink that balances the global
evaporation source at $1.72\times10^{10}$~kg~s$^{-1}$ with near-zero net storage. The
data close the global water budget to within a few hundredths of a percent in the mean.
Any persistent model drift of order 10\% is therefore a property of the model, not of
the target.

\section{The failure: a feedback loop the optimizer exploits}\label{sec:failure}

The corrector was enabled during a fine-tuning phase, applied to the predicted state
before the loss was computed. In code terms, the corrector rescaled precipitation in
place and the supervised error was then evaluated on the rescaled field. Over 12 epochs
the global water-budget drift rose monotonically from about 2\% to a plateau near 23\%
(Fig.~\ref{fig:drift}). The target value is 0.024\%. A second, independent training run
in the same configuration reproduced the failure and drifted further, to roughly 45\%,
which shows the behavior is a property of the configuration rather than of a single run.

The mechanism is a closed feedback loop between the corrector and the optimizer
(Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic}b). The corrector multiplies predicted precipitation by the
closure factor $r$. When raw precipitation is too low to close the budget, $r$ exceeds 1
and the corrector scales precipitation up. The loss, computed on the corrected field,
then sees precipitation that slightly overshoots the target and produces a gradient that
lowers raw precipitation further. Lower raw precipitation enlarges the residual, which
raises $r$, which scales the next prediction up more. The system converges to a
degenerate equilibrium in which raw precipitation is far too low, the closure factor is
large, and the corrected precipitation sits near the target. At the 23\% plateau the
corrector was inflating precipitation by roughly a quarter, implying a raw precipitation
sink about 18\% below the reference.

Two design choices made this possible. First, gradients flowed through the corrector:
because the loss was a function of the corrected output, and the correction depended on
the network output through $r$, the optimizer could reduce the loss by manipulating the
input to the corrector rather than by improving the physics. Second, nothing in the loss
rewarded genuine closure. The corrector computed the budget imbalance but the training
objective never included it, so a large closure factor carried no penalty.

This failure is dangerous because it hides. A diagnostic computed on the corrected
field, the quantity a user would naturally monitor, reported a closed budget throughout,
since closure is what the corrector guarantees by construction. The degradation was
visible only in the closure factor itself and in the raw, pre-correction precipitation.
Evaluating the corrected output confirms only that the corrector ran.

\section{The fix: keep the corrector out of the gradient path, and penalize the
imbalance}\label{sec:fix}

Two responses are available, and they are not equivalent
(Fig.~\ref{fig:schematic} contrasts the unconstrained, feedback, and decoupled cases).

The first is to disable the corrector during training and apply it only at inference.
This removes the feedback loop and is the least invasive option. It forgoes any benefit
of training-time conservation, and prior work on physics-constrained emulators reports
that conservation learned during training can improve fields beyond what an
inference-only correction achieves \citep{fuxiphys}. We did not measure that trade-off
here.

The second response keeps the corrector in place but stops gradients from flowing
through it. The supervised loss is computed on the pre-correction prediction, so the
optimizer drives raw precipitation toward the target directly and never optimizes against
the corrector. The budget imbalance is then added as a separate penalty, proportional to
the squared closure deviation. Because $r-1$ equals the budget residual divided by the
global precipitation sink, this penalty is the normalized global imbalance of the
network's uncorrected output. The soft penalty and the hard corrector then enforce the
same constraint on the same field, differing only in that the penalty rewards closure
while the corrector imposes it. This is a hybrid of the loss-constrained and
architecture-constrained formulations of \citet{beucler2021}: the soft penalty shapes the
learned solution during training, and the hard corrector is retained to guarantee exact
closure of the delivered field at inference. Structurally it cannot juice the residual,
because no path exists from the closure factor to the supervised gradient. In a
continuation run from the degenerate checkpoint, at a penalty weight of 0.1, the
decoupling returned the drift from 23\% to below 1\% within a single epoch and held it
near zero for the following 64 epochs, with a mean of 0.11\% and a standard deviation of
0.83\% over the final five epochs.

We recommend the second response as the principled solution: it removes the feedback path
by construction, and it is the configuration we have shown to be stable. What separates it
from the failing configuration is two changes: the budget imbalance enters the loss as a
penalty rather than being computed and discarded, and the supervised gradient is kept off
the corrected field. The penalty is what converts the corrector from a quantity the
optimizer games into a constraint the optimizer respects. A third option, supervising the
corrected output directly while relying on the penalty to contain the feedback, is
discussed in Section~\ref{sec:lesson} as a direction we did not pursue.

Two implementation details proved necessary for the diagnosis rather than the fix.
Global budget integrals must be accumulated in double precision. The budget terms are
differences of large area-weighted sums over roughly 55{,}000 grid cells, so the drift is
sensitive to the accumulation precision. Single-precision accumulation is already
adequate (pairwise summation keeps the float32 error negligible); the error appears at
the bfloat16 precision used for mixed-precision training, where the drift error reaches a
few tenths of a percent (mean 0.22\%, up to 0.52\% across 30 sampled steps), comparable to
the natural per-step spread and large enough to obscure small genuine drifts. Casting the
sums to float64 removes it. And the corrector must run as an explicit step whose imbalance
is returned to the training loop, rather than as an opaque in-line operation, so that the
penalty term can be formed at all.

\section{The generalizable lesson}\label{sec:lesson}

Hard correctors and soft penalties enforce the same constraint in different places, and
the placement determines whether end-to-end training is stable. A soft penalty acts on
the loss surface: it adds a gradient that points toward the constraint, and the network
moves with it. A hard corrector acts on the output: it removes the constraint violation
after the fact, and if that corrected output then feeds the loss, the corrector becomes
part of the computational graph the optimizer searches. The optimizer will exploit any
degree of freedom the corrector exposes, including the freedom to offload error onto the
correction.

The lesson for AI weather and climate emulators is therefore specific and actionable. A
hard physical-constraint correction may be used freely at inference, but during training
it must be kept out of the gradient path between the network and the supervised target.
If a constraint is to influence training, it should enter as a penalty on the constraint
violation, which is the soft formulation, not as a correction of the field that the
supervised loss evaluates. The two strategies can be combined, with the corrector
guaranteeing exact closure of the delivered field and the penalty shaping the learned
solution, provided the supervised loss is taken on the uncorrected prediction. This
hybrid is, in short, a hard constraint that is trained softly. The two parts do not act
in the same place at the same time. Under the decoupling, the hard correction
contributes nothing to the gradient during training and matters only for the delivered
field and at inference, while the soft penalty carries all of the constraint's influence
on learning.

A corollary concerns evaluation, and it may be the lesson most likely to be missed: a
global conservation diagnostic is not a check on the model when a hard corrector is
present, because the corrector guarantees closure by construction. Monitoring the
corrected budget reported nothing wrong for twelve epochs while the prediction degraded.
The informative quantities are the magnitude of the correction the model requires and the
physical fidelity of the pre-correction field; a closed corrected budget can conceal a
model in free fall.

These points apply wherever a deterministic projection or rescaling enforces a global
invariant on a trained emulator: mass and surface-pressure fixers, energy-balance
fixers, moisture fixers, and tracer non-negativity clamps all expose a correction factor
that an end-to-end optimizer can manipulate if the loss sees the corrected output.

Our result should be read alongside \citet{sha2025}, who integrate global mass, water,
and energy conservation into an AIWP model during training and inference and report
improved skill, reduced precipitation (drizzle) bias, and a stabilization effect that
grows with forecast lead time. That work establishes the benefit of training-time
conservation; the present finding identifies a failure mode in how the correction is
coupled to the loss, and the two are consistent. Their baseline over-predicted
precipitation and the water scheme reduced it, whereas our model under-predicted and a
loss on the corrected output drove it lower still; the sign of the outcome depends on the
initial bias and on whether the supervised gradient passes through the corrector. Keeping
the corrector out of that gradient path (Eq.~\eqref{eq:total}) is what lets the
conservation benefit be realized without the feedback. The regime where \citet{sha2025}
report the largest gains, long free-running rollouts, is also where our per-step training
diagnostics are silent: we verify closure at each training step but not over a
multi-decade integration, and their stabilization effect is the natural target for that
test.

We can only speculate on why the raw prediction biased low to begin with, giving the
feedback a direction to amplify. Mean-squared-error training is subject to the
double-penalty problem in precipitation: a sharp but displaced precipitation feature is
penalized both where it is predicted and where it is observed, so the loss rewards
smoother, weaker precipitation, the same tendency that produces drizzle bias and that
\citet{sha2025} verify with SEEPS rather than RMSE. This pre-existing bias plausibly seeds
the under-prediction that the conservation feedback then amplifies into the roughly 25\%
correction at plateau; the feedback supplies the amplification, not the initial direction.
Isolating the two would require training with a displacement-aware precipitation loss,
which we did not do.

A speculative variant is worth noting as future work. One could supervise the corrected
output directly, which a production model arguably should since that is the field it
delivers, while relying on the conservation penalty to contain the feedback rather than
remove it. This keeps the corrector in the gradient path, trading the structural guarantee
of the decoupled loss for a tuning-dependent balance; a single exploratory run in this
configuration was unstable in our hands, though under conditions too confounded to draw a
firm conclusion. The one element of that variant clearly worth keeping, an additional
conservation penalty applied at intermediate steps of a multi-step rollout, is independent
of where the supervised loss is taken and can be added on top of the decoupled loss
without reintroducing the coupling. The productive path is therefore the decoupled loss
augmented with such penalties; a controlled comparison of corrected-output supervision is
left to future work.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}

The evidence comes from a single emulator, a single physical constraint, and a small
number of training configurations rather than a controlled comparison across models and
constraints. The drift trajectory and the recovery are drawn from training-log
diagnostics of production runs, not from a designed experiment with held-out evaluation.
The decoupled penalty formulation was stable over 64 training epochs, but this is drift
measured per training step on teacher-forced data; we have not verified that the budget
remains closed over a long free-running climate integration, where slow drift would
matter most. We report the behavior of the global water budget and its closure, and
deliberately do not claim an improvement in forecast skill or climate fidelity, which
require separate evaluation. The reference budget is stationary across the five years we
evaluated (1980--1984): the annual mean drift stays within 0.022\% to 0.029\% and the
standard deviation within 2.04\% to 2.29\%, so the near-zero baseline is not specific to a
single year. Finally, we did not measure the inference-only baseline against the
training-time penalty, so the claim that training-time conservation helps rests on prior
work rather than on results presented here.

\appendix
\appendixtitle{The conservation-coupled training loss}

Let $\hat y$ be the network prediction and $y$ the target next state. The supervised term is
a latitude- and variable-weighted error,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sup}}(y,\hat y)=\frac{1}{V}\sum_{v=1}^{V}
\frac{1}{B\,H\,W}\sum_{b,i,j} w_v\, L^{\mathrm{lat}}_{i}\,
\ell\!\left(y_{b,v,ij},\hat y_{b,v,ij}\right),
\end{equation}
with per-variable weights $w_v$, latitude weights $L^{\mathrm{lat}}_i=\cos(\varphi_i)^p/
\overline{\cos(\varphi)^p}$, and a pointwise error $\ell$ (here mean squared error), summed
over batch $B$, latitude $H$, and longitude $W$.

The water corrector forms three globally integrated, area-weighted ($a_{ij}$) quantities
per sample $b$: the total-column-water tendency, the evaporation source, and the
precipitation sink,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{T}_b=\!\sum_{ij} a_{ij}\frac{\mathrm{TWC}^{\mathrm{pred}}_{b,ij}-\mathrm{TWC}^{\mathrm{in}}_{b,ij}}{\Delta t},\quad
\mathcal{E}_b=\!\sum_{ij} a_{ij}\frac{E_{b,ij}\rho_w}{\Delta t},\quad
\mathcal{P}_b=\!\sum_{ij} a_{ij}\frac{P_{b,ij}\rho_w}{\Delta t},
\end{equation}
where $\Delta t$ is the step length and $\rho_w$ the density of water. These sums are
accumulated in float64 (Section~\ref{sec:fix}). The budget residual and the multiplicative
closure factor that the corrector applies to precipitation are
\begin{equation}
R_b=-\mathcal{T}_b-\mathcal{E}_b-\mathcal{P}_b,\qquad
r_b=\frac{\mathcal{P}_b+R_b}{\mathcal{P}_b},\qquad
r_b-1=\frac{R_b}{\mathcal{P}_b}.
\end{equation}
The corrector rescales precipitation as $P\!\to\!r_b\,P$, which closes the delivered budget
exactly. The conservation penalty is the mean squared closure deviation, equivalently the
normalized budget imbalance of the uncorrected field,
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{water}}=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\left(r_b-1\right)^2
=\frac{1}{B}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\left(\frac{R_b}{\mathcal{P}_b}\right)^2 .
\end{equation}
The total objective at a back-propagated step is
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sup}}\!\left(y,\hat y_{\mathrm{pre}}\right)
+\lambda_w\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{water}},
\label{eq:total}
\end{equation}
where $\hat y_{\mathrm{pre}}$ is the \emph{pre-correction} prediction and
$\lambda_w$ is the penalty weight. The failing configuration is recovered by evaluating
$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sup}}$ on the corrected output $r_b\,\hat y$ instead of
$\hat y_{\mathrm{pre}}$: the supervised gradient then flows through $r_b$ and the optimizer
can reduce $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sup}}$ by lowering raw precipitation, which inflates $r_b$.
Equation~\eqref{eq:total} removes that path. Mass and energy correctors compute analogous
factors but contribute no term to $\mathcal{L}$.
